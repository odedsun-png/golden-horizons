# Medicare Doesn't Follow You Abroad — Here Are 5 Alternatives

> **THE SHORT ANSWER:** Medicare stops at the U.S. border — with narrow exceptions for emergencies near Canada or Mexico. That's not a loophole; it's a wall. But five real alternatives exist, ranging from ~$42/month travel medical policies to $350+/month expat plans that work like stateside PPOs. The right pick depends on whether you're spending three weeks in Lisbon or three years in Chiang Mai.

## Retirement Snapshot

| Factor | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| **Primary decision** | Which of five coverage types fits your abroad timeline and budget |
| **Best fit** | Retirees planning 30+ days outside the U.S. who can't afford a six-figure medical bill |
| **Biggest warning** | A Medigap foreign-travel benefit caps at $50,000 lifetime — one hospital stay in Europe can exceed that |
| **Cost range** | $42–$150/month (travel medical) to $250–$550/month (full expat plan, age 65+) |
| **Medicare trap** | If you disenroll from Part B while abroad, you face a 10% premium penalty for every 12 months you were unenrolled — permanently |

## Medicare Abroad: What Actually Counts and What Doesn't

Here's what Medicare.gov says in plain language:

**Original Medicare (Parts A & B)** pays for healthcare services outside the U.S. in only three narrow situations:

1. You're on a ship within six hours of a U.S. port and need emergency care
2. You're traveling between Alaska and the lower 48 states, and a Canadian hospital is closer than a U.S. one
3. You live near the Mexican or Canadian border, and the nearest hospital is across the line

That's it. No routine care. No prescriptions. No specialist visits. Not in Paris, not in Panama City, not in Puerto Vallarta.

**Medigap (Medicare Supplement) Plans C, D, F, G, M, and N** include a foreign travel emergency benefit — but read the fine print:

- Covers 80% of emergency costs after a $250 deductible
- Lifetime cap: **$50,000** (a single ICU stay in Germany or Japan can burn through that)
- Only covers the first 60 days of a trip
- Does NOT cover routine care, prescriptions, or pre-existing conditions abroad
- Plan F is closed to new enrollees who became Medicare-eligible after January 1, 2020

**Medicare Advantage (Part C)** plans vary wildly. UnitedHealthcare and Humana offer some plans with limited worldwide emergency coverage (typically 30–90 days, emergencies only). But most MA plans offer zero foreign coverage, and none cover routine care abroad. Check your Summary of Benefits — if "foreign travel emergency" isn't listed, it's not there.

**Bottom line:** If you're spending more than a few weeks abroad — or planning to live overseas — Medicare alone leaves you dangerously exposed.

## The 5 Alternatives, Ranked by Use Case

### Alternative 1: Travel Medical Insurance — Best for Trips Under 6 Months

**What it is:** Short-term health insurance designed for travelers. Covers emergencies, hospitalization, and medical evacuation while abroad.

**Named providers and real pricing (age 65–70, 2026 rates):**

| Provider | Monthly cost | Coverage limit | Evacuation | Pre-existing conditions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IMG Global (Patriot International)** | $42–$85 | Up to $1,000,000 | Included | Acute onset only |
| **GeoBlue Voyager** | $75–$130 | $250,000 | Up to $500,000 | Acute onset after 5-day wait |
| **WorldTrips Atlas Travel** | $50–$95 | Up to $2,000,000 | Included | Acute onset only |
| **Allianz Travel Insurance** | $60–$110 | $50,000–$500,000 | Up to $1,000,000 | Limited |

**What "acute onset" means:** A sudden, unexpected flare-up of a pre-existing condition — like a heart attack in someone with managed hypertension. Chronic management, refills, specialist follow-ups, and ongoing treatment are NOT covered.

**Best for:** Snowbirds spending 2–6 months abroad. Travelers who keep U.S. residency and Part B active.

**Watch out for:** Most policies won't cover you past age 80. Rates jump significantly after 70. Always check the policy's "look-back period" for pre-existing conditions (typically 60–180 days).

### Alternative 2: Annual Multi-Trip Plans — Best for Frequent Travelers

**What it is:** A single annual policy covering unlimited trips, typically capped at 30–90 days per trip.

**Named providers:**

- **GeoBlue Trekker Choice** — covers unlimited trips up to 70 days each, $150,000 per trip, ~$1,200–$2,400/year (age 65–75)
- **IMG Global iTravelInsured Travel Medical** — similar structure, $100,000–$500,000 coverage, ~$900–$1,800/year

**Best for:** Retirees who take 3–6 international trips per year but maintain a U.S. home base.

### Alternative 3: International Health Insurance / Expat Plans — Best for Living Abroad Full-Time

**What it is:** Comprehensive health insurance designed for expatriates — works like a PPO but worldwide. Covers routine care, specialists, prescriptions, hospitalization, and emergencies.

**Named providers and real pricing (age 65–70, 2026 rates):**

| Provider | Monthly cost | Deductible options | Inpatient | Outpatient | Prescriptions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cigna Global** | $350–$550 | $0–$5,000 | Yes | Yes (add-on) | Yes (add-on) |
| **Aetna International** | $300–$500 | $250–$2,500 | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| **GeoBlue Xplorer** | $400–$600 | $250–$5,000 | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| **Allianz Care** | $280–$450 | $500–$5,000 | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| **IMG Global Medical Insurance** | $250–$400 | $1,000–$10,000 | Yes | Optional | Optional |

**Key differences from travel medical:**
- Covers **routine care** — annual physicals, bloodwork, specialist referrals
- Covers **pre-existing conditions** after a waiting period (typically 12–24 months) or with medical underwriting
- No per-trip time limits — designed for people living abroad indefinitely
- Direct billing at partner hospitals in most countries (no out-of-pocket then reimbursement)

**The Part B penalty trap:** If you suspend Part B while abroad, you'll pay a **10% late enrollment penalty for every full 12 months** you were unenrolled — for life. At 2026 Part B premiums of ~$185/month, that adds ~$18.50/month permanently after just one year away. Many expats keep Part B active as a safety net even while paying for international coverage — budget for both.

**Best for:** Full-time expats who have moved abroad and need comprehensive, year-round coverage.

### Alternative 4: Local National Health Systems — Best for Legal Residents of Specific Countries

**What it is:** Many countries offer public or semi-public healthcare that foreign residents can access — sometimes free, sometimes for a monthly fee.

**Real examples:**

| Country | Program | Cost | What's included | Eligibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mexico** | IMSS Seguro Facultativo | ~$500–$600/year (~$45/month) | Primary care, specialists, hospitalization, prescriptions | Legal residents with temporary or permanent visa |
| **Spain** | Convenio Especial | ~€60/month (under 65), ~€157/month (65+) | Full public system access | Legal residents registered in the Padrón |
| **Portugal** | SNS | Free for legal residents | Primary care, ER, specialists (with wait times) | NIF number + registered address |
| **Thailand** | Private system only | N/A for foreigners | N/A — expats use private hospitals | Bumrungrad ER visit: ~$150–$300 |
| **Costa Rica** | CCSS (Caja) | ~$80–$120/month (income-based) | Full public system | Legal residents with cédula |

**The catch:** Quality, wait times, and language barriers vary enormously. Most Golden Horizons readers pair local public coverage with a private supplemental plan for faster access and English-speaking providers.

**Best for:** Retirees with legal residency who want affordable baseline coverage and are comfortable navigating a foreign healthcare system.

### Alternative 5: Medical Evacuation Insurance — Essential Add-On for Everyone

**What it is:** Covers the cost of emergency air transport to the nearest adequate facility — or back to the U.S. — if you're seriously injured or ill abroad.

**Why it matters:** A medical evacuation flight from Southeast Asia to the U.S. costs **$100,000–$300,000**. From Central America: $25,000–$75,000. From Europe: $50,000–$150,000. Without coverage, that's your bill.

**Named providers:**

**Best for:** Every retiree who spends time abroad. This is the one coverage you should never skip, regardless of what other insurance you carry.

## The Trade-Off

**What you gain:** Freedom to live or travel abroad without risking financial ruin from a medical emergency. Access to quality healthcare in countries where a GP visit costs $15–$50 instead of $250+.

**What you give up:** The simplicity of one system. Expat plans cost $250–$550/month on top of the ~$185/month Part B premium most people keep active. You become your own benefits administrator — filing claims, tracking deductibles, managing coverage gaps during moves.

## The Golden Horizons 30-Day Test

1. **Call your Medigap insurer this week** and ask for written confirmation of your foreign travel emergency benefit — the dollar cap, the per-trip day limit, and whether it covers your planned destination.

2. **Get three travel medical quotes** from IMG, GeoBlue, and WorldTrips for a 90-day trip to your target country. Compare the pre-existing condition clauses word by word — "acute onset" definitions vary between carriers.

3. **Request a Cigna Global or Aetna International quote** for a full-year expat plan. Ask specifically about the waiting period for pre-existing conditions and whether your current medications are on their formulary.

4. **Research your destination's public system.** If you're considering Mexico, call the nearest IMSS office about Seguro Facultativo enrollment. For Spain, check convenio especial eligibility at your local Centro de Salud. For Portugal, ask about SNS registration at the nearest centro de saúde.

5. **Price evacuation coverage** from Medjet ($315–$430/year) and compare it to the evacuation benefit in the travel medical quotes — some policies include evacuation, others don't.

6. **Calculate the Part B penalty math.** Multiply the number of years you'd suspend Part B by 10% of the current premium (~$18.50/month per year in 2026). Compare that lifetime penalty cost against the savings from suspending — for most people, keeping Part B active while abroad is cheaper long-term.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Original Medicare cover any healthcare services abroad?

Only in three narrow situations: emergency care on a ship within six hours of a U.S. port, care at a Canadian hospital when traveling between Alaska and the lower 48, or care at a border hospital when you live closer to a foreign hospital than a U.S. one. Outside those three scenarios, Original Medicare pays $0 for care abroad. (Source: medicare.gov)

### What happens to my Part B if I move abroad — can I re-enroll later?

You can disenroll from Part B while abroad, but re-enrollment is limited to the General Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31 each year), with coverage starting July 1. You'll also face a permanent 10% premium surcharge for every full 12 months you were unenrolled — at 2026 rates, that's ~$18.50/month extra for each year, added to your premium for life. (Source: cms.gov)

### How much does international health insurance cost for a 67-year-old retiree?

Comprehensive expat plans from Cigna Global, Aetna International, or GeoBlue Xplorer run $300–$550/month for a 67-year-old, depending on deductible ($0–$5,000) and whether outpatient/prescription add-ons are included. Travel medical insurance for shorter stays is cheaper: $42–$130/month from IMG or WorldTrips. (Source: provider rate tables, 2026)

### Can I use a foreign country's public healthcare system as a retiree?

It depends on the country and your legal status. Mexico's IMSS Seguro Facultativo costs ~$500–$600/year for legal residents. Spain's convenio especial runs €157/month for those 65+. Portugal's SNS is free for registered residents. Thailand has no public option for foreigners — you'll need private insurance. Each requires legal residency, not just a tourist visa. (Source: country health ministry websites)

### Is medical evacuation insurance worth the cost?

At $315–$430/year from Medjet, it's the cheapest peace-of-mind purchase in retirement planning. A medical evacuation flight from Southeast Asia to the U.S. runs $100,000–$300,000; from Latin America, $25,000–$75,000. One emergency flight without coverage could wipe out years of retirement savings. (Source: Medjet, Global Rescue published rates)
