# Strategy - How to Handle Your US Healthcare Before Moving Abroad — A Local's Perspective

Approximately 3.6 million Americans currently live abroad, and many of them are retirees. One of the most critical considerations for these retirees is how to manage their U.S. healthcare before making the move. Transitioning healthcare coverage from the U.S. to an international setting can be complex, involving Medicare, private insurance, and local healthcare systems. This article will guide you through the steps necessary to ensure your healthcare needs are met before you relocate.

## Quick Take

- **Best for:** American retirees who want a seamless transition in healthcare coverage when moving abroad.
- **Monthly reality:** Budget for potential dual coverage costs and international insurance premiums.
- **Biggest advantage:** Peace of mind knowing your healthcare needs are covered both in the U.S. and abroad.
- **Biggest warning:** Potential gaps in coverage if not planned correctly.
- **Before moving:** Verify visa, healthcare, tax, housing, and insurance rules with official sources or a qualified professional.

## Understanding Medicare and Its Limitations Abroad

![photo](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1500277127996-2e6206b069ef?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5NDQ1NTl8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwxMXx8VVNBfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODM1MDAwNDN8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

Medicare, the U.S. federal health insurance program, does not typically cover healthcare services outside the United States. While there are some exceptions, such as emergency services in Canada or during travel between the U.S. and Alaska, these are limited. It's crucial to understand that relying solely on Medicare while living abroad could leave you without adequate healthcare coverage.

For retirees who plan to return to the U.S. periodically, maintaining Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) is often advisable, as it is generally premium-free for those who have paid into the system for at least ten years. However, Medicare Part B (medical insurance) requires a monthly premium and should be carefully considered. Dropping Part B to save costs while living abroad might seem tempting, but re-enrollment can be complicated and more expensive later.

One practical solution is to maintain Medicare coverage while also securing international health insurance. This dual coverage ensures that you have access to healthcare services both abroad and during visits to the U.S. However, this approach can be costly, as international health insurance premiums vary widely depending on factors like age, health status, and coverage level.

## Choosing the Right International Health Insurance

![photo](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1473090826765-d54ac2fdc1eb?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5NDQ1NTl8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyMXx8VVNBfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3ODM1MDAwNDN8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

International health insurance is essential for retirees moving abroad, as it provides coverage for medical services outside the U.S. Companies like Cigna, Aetna International, and Allianz offer plans tailored to expatriates, with varying levels of coverage, including emergency services, hospitalization, and routine check-ups.

When selecting a plan, consider the healthcare standards and costs in your destination country. Some countries, like Mexico or Portugal, offer high-quality healthcare at lower costs than the U.S., which might influence your insurance needs. Conversely, in countries with higher healthcare costs, such as Switzerland or Japan, comprehensive coverage is advisable.

Be aware of potential exclusions or limitations in international plans. Pre-existing conditions, for example, might not be covered immediately or could require additional premiums. Additionally, some plans might exclude coverage for certain treatments or medications, so it's vital to read the policy details carefully.

Retirees should also evaluate the insurance provider's network of healthcare facilities and professionals in their destination country. Having access to a broad network ensures that you can receive care from reputable providers without facing hefty out-of-pocket expenses.

## Managing U.S. Healthcare Ties

For retirees who plan to maintain ties to the U.S., such as property ownership or regular visits, managing U.S. healthcare connections is crucial. This includes understanding how to handle existing prescriptions, medical records, and doctor relationships.

Before moving, request a comprehensive copy of your medical records from your U.S. healthcare providers. These records will be invaluable when establishing care abroad, as they provide a history of your health conditions, treatments, and medications. Additionally, if you have ongoing prescriptions, discuss with your doctor the possibility of obtaining a larger supply before the move or transitioning to equivalent medications available in your new country.

Another consideration is maintaining a relationship with a U.S.-based healthcare provider. Telemedicine services have become more prevalent and could offer a convenient way to consult with U.S. doctors while living abroad. This can be particularly beneficial for managing chronic conditions or when seeking a second opinion.

Finally, consider setting up a U.S. mailing address for healthcare-related correspondence. This could be a trusted family member's address or a mail forwarding service, ensuring you receive important healthcare communications without delay.

## Healthcare, Visa, Tax, and Safety Notes

Retirees should verify healthcare access in their destination country, including the need for private insurance and the quality of local medical facilities. Understand visa or residency rules that might affect healthcare eligibility. Additionally, consider both U.S. and local tax obligations, as these can impact your financial planning and healthcare budget.

Safety and walkability are crucial factors, especially if you anticipate needing regular medical visits. Ensure that your new home is in a safe area with easy access to healthcare services and emergency care.

## Common Mistakes Retirees Should Avoid

- Dropping Medicare Part B without understanding re-enrollment penalties and costs.
- Overlooking the need for international health insurance.
- Failing to obtain and transfer comprehensive medical records.
- Ignoring the importance of a U.S. mailing address for healthcare correspondence.
- Underestimating the cost of dual healthcare coverage.
- Not researching the quality and availability of local healthcare services.
- Forgetting to verify visa or residency requirements that impact healthcare access.

## Before You Move Checklist

- Verify visa or residency rules affecting healthcare eligibility.
- Research healthcare access and quality in your destination country.
- Obtain international health insurance coverage.
- Maintain or evaluate Medicare coverage options.
- Compile and transfer medical records to your new healthcare provider.
- Set up a U.S. mailing address for healthcare communications.
- Discuss prescription needs with your current doctor.
- Ensure access to telemedicine services if needed.
- Budget for dual coverage costs and potential out-of-pocket expenses.
- Check local transportation options for medical visits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Medicare cover healthcare services abroad?

Medicare generally does not cover healthcare services outside the U.S., with limited exceptions. Retirees should consider maintaining Medicare for U.S. visits and obtaining international health insurance for coverage abroad.

### What should I consider when choosing international health insurance?

Evaluate the healthcare standards and costs in your destination country, and choose a plan that covers necessary services, including emergency care and hospitalization. Verify coverage for pre-existing conditions and network availability.

### How can I manage my U.S. healthcare connections while living abroad?

Request comprehensive medical records, set up a U.S. mailing address, and consider telemedicine services to maintain connections with U.S.-based healthcare providers. Discuss prescription needs with your doctor before moving.
