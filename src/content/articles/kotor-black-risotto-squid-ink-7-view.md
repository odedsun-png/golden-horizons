## Quick Taste

A plate of black risotto in Kotor costs roughly what you'd pay for a side dish at an American chain restaurant.

The dish — crni rizot — is rice cooked in squid ink with Adriatic mussels, sometimes cuttlefish or prawns, finished with garlic, white wine and olive oil. It arrives jet-black, briny, and surprisingly creamy. It stains your teeth for about ten minutes. It is also one of the best things you can eat in Montenegro for under €10.

At restaurants inside Kotor's medieval walls, black risotto typically runs €7 to €12. Add a glass of local white wine for €3 to €4, a basket of bread that often arrives without asking, and you're looking at a full waterfront lunch for roughly what a single entree costs at Applebee's.

For anyone evaluating Montenegro as a place to spend more time, a €7 risotto with a view of the Bay of Kotor is the kind of data point that matters more than a resort brochure.

## The Story Behind the Dish

Crni rizot started as fishermen's food. The crew came back with squid and mussels, the ink went into the pot because wasting it made no sense, and the rice stretched the catch into a full meal. That's the entire origin story — practical, not romantic.

The dish spread across the eastern Adriatic coast. Croatia, Montenegro and parts of Italy each have their own version. Kotor's leans heavier on mussels, which are harvested in the Bay of Kotor and tend to be plump and slightly sweet compared to Atlantic varieties.

What makes the Kotor version worth trying is the setting as much as the cooking. You're eating ink-black rice inside a walled town that's been standing since the 12th century, looking out at water ringed by mountains. The dish didn't need to be invented for tourists. It was already here.

## What Should You Order?

Start with the black risotto. That's the point.

At most restaurants in Kotor's Old Town, a standard portion runs €7 to €12. Some places serve it as a starter in a smaller bowl; others plate it as a full main course. Ask before ordering if the size isn't clear.

Beyond the risotto, worth knowing:

* **Buzara** — mussels or shrimp cooked in wine, garlic and tomato, served with bread for soaking. Usually €8 to €14.
* **Grilled squid** — whole, scored, charred. Around €9 to €13.
* **Njeguski steak** — stuffed with smoked ham and local cheese from the village of Njeguzi. €10 to €14. Not seafood, but distinctly Montenegrin.
* **Shopska salad** — tomato, cucumber, onion, grated white cheese. €3 to €5.

For wine, ask for Krstac (a local Montenegrin white) rather than defaulting to an imported label. It pairs well with seafood and usually costs €3 to €4 per glass.

## The Dish Worth Knowing: Cataplana vs. Buzara

If the Algarve has cataplana, Montenegro has buzara.

Both are one-pot seafood preparations meant for sharing. Buzara is lighter — shellfish simmered in white wine, garlic, olive oil and sometimes a touch of tomato. It arrives in the pot it was cooked in, with bread on the side for the broth.

If you're traveling with someone, order one buzara and one black risotto. You'll cover the two most characteristic dishes of Montenegrin coastal cooking for under €25 total.

## Where to Find It

Inside Kotor's Old Town walls, restaurants line the narrow stone streets and open into small squares. Most serve black risotto. A few worth checking:

**Konoba Scala Santa** — tucked into the Old Town, known for traditional preparations. Small space, so it fills up.

**Galion** — waterfront, just outside the Old Town walls. You eat facing the bay. Slightly higher prices for the location, but the view is the real draw.

**Restaurant Bastion** — near the fortress walls, outdoor seating. Good for a longer, slower meal.

Prices at all three generally fall in the €7 to €15 range for risotto, depending on portion and extras.

For cheaper options, walk five minutes outside the Old Town gate toward the local market area. Smaller restaurants and bakeries there serve the same food with less foot traffic and lower markups.

## Why a €7 Risotto Matters

Montenegro is not on most Americans' retirement shortlist yet. That's part of what makes it interesting.

The country uses the euro despite not being in the EU. Healthcare costs are low. The Bay of Kotor has been compared to the Norwegian fjords. A one-bedroom apartment in Kotor rents for roughly €400 to €600 per month. And a plate of risotto costs €7.

None of that means Montenegro is the right fit for everyone. The bureaucracy can be slow. English is less widely spoken outside tourist areas. Winters are wet. The residency process has its own friction.

But food prices tell you something real about a place's cost structure. When a full seafood lunch with wine costs €12, that's not a fluke — it reflects what the local economy actually looks like.

## What to Know Before You Go

**Bread and cover charges.** Some restaurants add a small cover charge (€1 to €2) or charge for bread that arrives unasked. Check before assuming it's free.

**Cash vs. card.** Most Old Town restaurants accept cards. Smaller places outside the walls may prefer cash.

**Lunch vs. dinner pricing.** A few restaurants offer slightly lower lunch pricing or daily specials. Ask.

**Squid ink stains.** It will temporarily darken your teeth and lips. Water and bread fix it. Don't wear a white shirt.

**Portions vary.** "Black risotto" can mean anything from a starter-sized bowl to a full dinner plate. Confirm the size and whether mussels are included or extra.

## Common Mistakes Visitors Make

**Eating only inside the Old Town walls.** The restaurants immediately inside the gate charge a premium for convenience. Walk two minutes further and prices drop noticeably.

**Ordering black risotto at a pizza restaurant.** Stick to places that specialize in seafood or traditional Montenegrin cooking. A restaurant with 60 items on its menu is not making any of them particularly well.

**Skipping the local wine.** Imported wines cost more and miss the point. Krstac (white) and Vranac (red) are Montenegro's signature grapes. Try them.

**Confusing Kotor with Budva.** Budva is the party town 20 minutes south — louder, more crowded, higher prices. Kotor is quieter and more affordable. They are very different experiences.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does black risotto taste like?

Briny, savory and slightly sweet from the mussels. The squid ink gives it an earthy, oceanic depth without being fishy. The texture is creamy, closer to a thick stew than dry rice.

### How much does black risotto cost in Kotor?

Roughly €7 to €12 at most restaurants. Waterfront spots with prime views may charge €12 to €15.

### Is Kotor expensive compared to other European destinations?

No. It is significantly cheaper than Western Europe for dining, housing and daily expenses. It compares favorably to Portugal and is generally less expensive than Croatia.

### Can I get by with English in Kotor?

In the Old Town and tourist areas, yes. Outside those zones, basic phrases in Serbian or Montenegrin go a long way.

### Is Montenegro a realistic retirement destination for Americans?

It's emerging as one. Low costs, euro currency, Mediterranean climate and a residency permit process that is manageable but not effortless. Golden Horizons has covered it in more depth separately.
