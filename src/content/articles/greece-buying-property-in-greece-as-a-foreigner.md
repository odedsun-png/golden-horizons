# Greece - Buying Property in Greece as a Foreigner — A Local's Perspective

The Greek real estate market has experienced a notable increase in foreign interest, with property prices rising by 8% in 2022, according to the Bank of Greece. This surge is largely due to the country’s appealing climate, extensive history, and relatively affordable cost of living compared to other European countries. However, buying property in Greece as a foreigner involves dealing with a set of legal, financial, and logistical challenges. This article will guide you through the practicalities, benefits, and potential pitfalls of purchasing property in Greece.

## Quick Take

- **Best for:** American retirees who want a Mediterranean lifestyle with access to history and culture.
- **Monthly reality:** Expect a modest lifestyle in Greece to cost around $1,500 to $2,500, excluding property expenses.
- **Biggest advantage:** The opportunity to own a home in a beautiful location with potential for investment growth.
- **Biggest warning:** Complex bureaucracy and legal procedures can delay the buying process.
- **Before moving:** Verify visa, healthcare, tax, housing, and insurance rules with official sources or a qualified professional.

## Understanding the Greek Property Market

Property prices in central Athens, such as Kolonaki, average around €3,000 per square meter, while areas like Thessaloniki offer more affordable options at approximately €1,500 per square meter. The Greek property market is diverse, with opportunities ranging from city apartments to island villas. While Athens and Thessaloniki provide urban conveniences, the islands like Crete and Santorini offer picturesque settings at a premium.

However, purchasing property in Greece involves more than just finding the right location. The legal process can be intricate, requiring a Greek tax number (AFM) and a local bank account. Engaging a local lawyer is crucial to understanding the complexities of Greek property law. Additionally, non-EU citizens may face restrictions when purchasing property in border areas, which can include some islands.

## Legal and Financial Considerations

Obtaining a Greek tax number (AFM) is a prerequisite for buying property, which involves a visit to the local tax office. Another critical step is opening a Greek bank account to facilitate financial transactions. The Greek banking system has undergone significant reforms, but it can still be cumbersome for foreigners.

![photo](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1604145195376-e2c8195adf29?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3w5NDQ1NTl8MHwxfHNlYXJjaHwyMXx8R3JlZWNlfGVufDB8MHx8fDE3NzkwMDcyMTZ8MA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=1080)

When purchasing property, a Notary Public must be involved to ensure the transaction is legally binding. The notary's fee is typically around 1% of the property’s value. Additionally, consider the property transfer tax, which is approximately 3% of the purchase price, and other legal fees.

One practical warning: the Greek bureaucracy is notoriously slow, and it can take several months to complete a property purchase. Patience and persistence are crucial, as is the assistance of a qualified local lawyer who can guide you through the process.

## Lifestyle and Practical Challenges

Living in Greece offers a slower pace of life, which can be both a blessing and a challenge. While the Mediterranean lifestyle is appealing, with its emphasis on community and leisure, it can also mean slower service and less efficiency in day-to-day transactions.

Public transportation in major cities like Athens is relatively reliable, with buses, trams, and metro services. However, in rural areas and on islands, transport options can be limited, necessitating the use of a car. This can add to the cost of living and requires familiarity with local driving laws.

A common assumption is that English is widely spoken, but in many areas, particularly outside tourist zones, Greek is the primary language. This language barrier can pose challenges in everyday interactions and when dealing with official matters.

## Healthcare, Visa, Tax, and Safety Notes

Healthcare in Greece is a combination of public and private systems. While public healthcare is available, many expatriates opt for private insurance to avoid long wait times and ensure English-speaking medical staff. Verify the extent of your coverage and whether it meets your needs.

For non-EU citizens, obtaining a residency visa is necessary for long-term stays. This process can be complicated and requires proof of income, health insurance, and sometimes property ownership. Consult with Greek immigration authorities or a legal professional to ensure compliance with all residency requirements.

Tax obligations can be complex, particularly for those who plan to reside in Greece permanently. You'll need to understand both U.S. and Greek tax laws, as Greece has its own regulations regarding property and income tax. Professional advice is recommended to manage these obligations.

Safety in Greece is generally good, but like any country, it has areas with higher crime rates. Urban areas can experience petty crime, so it's wise to stay informed about local safety conditions.

## Common Mistakes Retirees Should Avoid

- Underestimating the complexity of Greek bureaucracy.
- Failing to engage a local lawyer for legal proceedings.
- Overlooking the necessity of a Greek tax number (AFM).
- Assuming all locals speak English fluently.
- Not budgeting for additional costs like property taxes and maintenance.
- Ignoring the need for a reliable local bank account.
- Overlooking transportation limitations in rural areas.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can foreigners buy property in Greece?

Yes, foreigners can buy property in Greece, but non-EU citizens may face restrictions in certain border areas. Consult with a local lawyer to understand any limitations.

### What are the costs associated with buying property in Greece?

In addition to the property price, expect to pay a property transfer tax of about 3%, notary fees around 1%, and legal fees. Additional costs may include property maintenance and taxes.

### Is healthcare accessible for retirees in Greece?

Greece offers both public and private healthcare options. Many retirees choose private insurance to ensure timely access and English-speaking staff. Verify the coverage that suits your needs.

### How is the cost of living in Greece compared to the U.S.?

Greece generally offers a lower cost of living than the U.S., particularly in terms of housing and daily expenses. However, this can vary significantly by region and lifestyle choices.

### What are the transportation options in Greece?

Major cities like Athens have reliable public transport, but rural areas may require a car. Consider transportation needs based on your chosen location.
