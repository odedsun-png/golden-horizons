## Quick Taste

In the lively heart of Quito's markets, hornado emerges as a savory spectacle. Picture a whole pig, slow-roasted to perfection, its skin crackling under the midday sun. This dish, a staple of Ecuadorian market stalls, offers succulent pork with a side of llapingachos (potato patties), mote (hominy), and a tangy salad. The pork is tender, the skin crispy, and the flavors richly layered, a testament to the Andean tradition. For about $4, you can enjoy this hearty meal, a price that reflects the everyday affordability of life in Ecuador, where a coffee might set you back just a dollar.

## The Story Behind the Dish

Hornado has deep roots in Ecuadorian culture, tracing back to indigenous culinary practices that prized the pig for its ability to feed many. Traditionally prepared for special occasions and gatherings, the dish symbolizes abundance and community. The method of slow-roasting the pig over a wood fire or in a clay oven imparts a unique flavor, a technique passed down through generations. The dish has evolved over time, with each region adding its own twist, but the essence remains the same: a celebration of local ingredients and communal eating.

## How Locals Eat It

For Ecuadorians, hornado is more than just a meal; it's an event. Locals often gather at markets on weekends, where the aroma of roasting pork fills the air. It's common to see families and friends sharing a platter, each taking their favorite cuts. The dish is typically served with agrio, a sauce made from fermented fruit, which adds a zingy contrast to the rich pork. Eating hornado is a leisurely affair, often accompanied by a cold beer or a glass of chicha, a traditional fermented corn drink. It's a time to relax, chat, and enjoy the simple pleasures of life.

## Where to Find It

While hornado can be found throughout Ecuador, the markets of Quito and Cuenca are particularly renowned for their offerings. In Quito, the Mercado Central is a must-visit, with vendors serving up generous portions of hornado alongside other local favorites. Cuenca's 10 de Agosto Market is another hotspot, where the dish is served with regional variations. For those looking to cook at home, local supermarkets and butcher shops sell the necessary cuts of pork and spices. Buying from these markets not only supports local farmers but also ensures the freshest ingredients.

## Regional Variations

Though hornado is a national dish, regional variations abound. In the highlands, the pork is often marinated with beer and achiote, a spice that gives the meat its characteristic color. Coastal versions might include a hint of citrus, reflecting the area's love for tangy flavors. In the south, some cooks add a splash of local liqueur to the marinade, giving the dish a distinct twist. Despite these differences, the core of hornado remains unchanged: tender pork, crispy skin, and a medley of sides that highlight the diversity of Ecuadorian produce.

## Cost, Portions, and Ordering Tips

A typical plate of hornado costs around $4, making it an affordable option for both locals and travelers. Portions are generous, often enough to satisfy even the heartiest appetites. When ordering, it's wise to specify your preferred cut of pork—some prefer the leaner loin, while others go for the fatty shoulder. Don't shy away from asking for extra llapingachos or mote, as these sides complement the meat perfectly. If you're dining in a larger group, consider ordering a whole platter to share, which often comes with a discount.

## What to Know Before You Try It

Hornado is best enjoyed fresh from the market, where the pork is roasted daily. Arrive early to ensure you get the best cuts, as the dish is popular and can sell out by afternoon. While the pork is the star, don't overlook the sides, which are essential to the experience. Be prepared for a bit of a wait during peak times, as vendors meticulously carve each portion. For those with dietary restrictions, note that the dish is traditionally heavy on meat and carbs, though some vendors offer vegetarian sides.

## Common Mistakes Visitors Make

One common mistake is underestimating the portion size. Hornado is meant to be hearty, so it's easy to over-order. Another is skipping the agrio sauce, which many first-timers overlook but is crucial for balancing the dish's richness. Visitors often stick to tourist markets, missing out on the more traditional experiences found in local neighborhoods. Finally, some travelers opt for pre-packaged versions from supermarkets, which lack the freshness and flavor of market-prepared hornado.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is hornado made of?
Hornado is primarily made of pork, slow-roasted until tender. It's typically served with sides like llapingachos, mote, and salad, and often accompanied by agrio sauce.

### Is hornado spicy?
Hornado itself is not spicy, but it is flavorful, with a mix of savory and tangy notes. The agrio sauce can add a slight kick, depending on the vendor.

### Can I find hornado outside of markets?
While markets are the best place to experience hornado, some restaurants offer it as a menu item, especially in cities like Quito and Cuenca.

### How is hornado different from other roast pork dishes?
Hornado is unique for its preparation method, using traditional Andean spices and cooking techniques that give it a distinct flavor profile compared to other roast pork dishes.
