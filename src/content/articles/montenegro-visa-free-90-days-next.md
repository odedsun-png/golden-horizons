# Montenegro Offers Visa-Free Entry for 90 Days — Then Here's What to Do

**Golden Horizons Verdict: 🟡 WORTH TESTING — VERIFY RESIDENCY**

> **THE SHORT ANSWER:** Americans can enter Montenegro visa-free for 90 days within any 180-day period — no application, no fee, just a valid passport. To stay beyond 90 days, you have three options: a D visa (up to 6 months, no work, must apply 60 days before travel), a property-based temporary residence permit (buy real estate assessed at €150,000+, €25 application fee, 30-day processing), or an income-based temporary residence permit (demonstrate pension income plus €3,650 in a Montenegrin bank account). All require a clean criminal record, valid health insurance, and police registration within 24 hours of arrival. Overstaying the 90-day limit risks fines and a re-entry ban of up to one year.

## Retirement Snapshot

**Primary decision:**
Whether to stay within the 90-day visa-free window (using Montenegro as a seasonal base) or commit to a longer-term residence permit that requires either property ownership or documented income.

**Best fit:**
Retirees who want to test Montenegro for 1-3 months before deciding, or those ready to buy property or document pension income for a renewable one-year permit.

**Biggest warning:**
Montenegro updated its Law on Foreigners in January 2026 — property-based residence now requires a minimum €150,000 tax-assessed value, and electronic applications through the new Visa Information System are mandatory. Rules are actively changing as Montenegro pursues EU accession (targeted by 2028). What's accurate today may shift within months.

**Planning-budget lens:**
The visa-free entry costs nothing. A D visa application takes 10-30 days to process. A temporary residence permit costs €25 in fees, €3,650 in a Montenegrin bank deposit (refundable), €500-1,000 for a local lawyer, and roughly €200-400 for translated/apostilled documents.

## Phase 1: The 90-Day Visa-Free Window

### How It Works

Americans enter Montenegro with a valid US passport — no visa application, no consulate visit, no fee. You get a passport stamp at the border and 90 days within a rolling 180-day window.

The 180-day clock starts on your first day of entry. Every day you spend in Montenegro counts against your 90-day allowance. Leave for a week, and those days outside Montenegro don't count — but they do count toward the 180-day window. You cannot reset the clock by crossing into Croatia for a coffee and coming back.

**Example:** You arrive January 1 and stay until March 31 (90 days). Your 180-day window runs January 1 through June 29. You cannot re-enter Montenegro until July 1 — even though you left on March 31, you've used all 90 days within that window.

### What You Must Do Within 24 Hours

Register your address with the local police — the prijava boravka, informally called the "white card." This is mandatory and enforced.

- **Hotels and licensed apartments** do this automatically at check-in.
- **Airbnb, private rentals, or staying with friends** — you or your host must register in person at a Turistički Info Centar or the local police station.
- **Tourist tax:** €1.00 per adult per day for the first 30 days of your stay. Children 12-18 pay €0.50. Exempt after 30 continuous days.

Data is entered into the RB90 electronic database and linked to your passport number. Border police can check your registration history when you leave.

**Fines for non-registration:** €200-600 per person if discovered at the border on departure. Your host faces €500-2,000 if they failed to register you.

### What Happens If You Overstay

Overstaying the 90-day limit triggers a fine, potential deportation, and a re-entry ban of up to one year from the date you leave Montenegro. The overstay is recorded in the system and increases scrutiny on any future visa or residence applications. This is not theoretical — Montenegro enforces it at land borders and the Podgorica and Tivat airports.

## Phase 2: Staying Beyond 90 Days — Three Paths

### Path A: The D Visa (Long-Stay Visa)

**What it is:** A visa allowing stays of more than 90 days but less than 6 months within a 12-month period. Issued for single, double, or multiple entries.

**Who it's for:** Retirees who want 4-6 months per year in Montenegro without buying property or applying for residence. Also used by digital nomads before the formal digital nomad program launched.

**How to apply:**
- Submit your application through Montenegro's electronic Visa Information System (VIS) at least 60 days before your intended travel date — this is new as of 2026.
- Apply at the Montenegrin embassy or consulate in your home country. The nearest for most Americans is the Embassy of Montenegro in Washington, D.C.
- Decision within 10 days, potentially extended to 30 days if additional review is needed.

**Required documents:**
- Valid passport (validity must cover your intended stay)
- Proof of accommodation in Montenegro (rental contract or hotel booking)
- Proof of financial means (bank statements, pension award letter)
- Valid health insurance covering your stay
- Clean criminal record certificate (issued within 6 months, translated and apostilled)
- Round-trip flight booking or travel itinerary

**Cost:** The visa fee varies by entry type — typically €50-100 for a single-entry D visa.

**Limitation:** A D visa does not allow you to work in Montenegro. It's a stay permit, not a residence permit. It does not count toward the 5-year path to permanent residency.

### Path B: Temporary Residence via Property Ownership

**What it is:** A one-year temporary residence permit based on owning real estate in Montenegro with a tax-assessed value of at least €150,000 (formalized under the January 2026 amendments to the Law on Foreigners).

**Who it's for:** Retirees who want to buy an apartment or house and use it as the basis for legal residency. The property can be rented out when you're not in Montenegro — there's no minimum-stay requirement.

**Key details:**
- The €150,000 threshold is the tax-assessed value, not the purchase price. Tax assessments often run below market value, which can work in your favor or against you.
- Permits issued before January 17, 2026 under the old rules (no minimum value) can be renewed without proving the €150,000 threshold.
- Your spouse and family members qualify under the same permit, including same-sex partners (expanded under the 2026 amendments).
- Renewable annually. After 5 continuous years of temporary residence, you can apply for permanent residency. Citizenship eligibility comes after 10 total years (5 temporary + 5 permanent).

**Required documents:**
- Valid passport (all pages copied)
- Property ownership deed (buying/selling contract registered with the Real Estate Administration)
- Tax assessment certificate confirming €150,000+ value
- Bank statement from a Montenegrin bank showing €3,650 deposit (can be withdrawn after the application is processed)
- Valid health insurance (minimum 30 days coverage)
- Criminal record certificate from your home country (issued within 6 months, translated by a licensed Montenegrin translator)
- Proof of paying the €25 residence permit fee

**Where to apply:** In person at the local police station (Uprava Policije) in the municipality where your property is located. Since 2026, the initial application can be submitted electronically with scanned documents, but you must appear in person within 10 days of entering Montenegro.

**Processing time:** 30 days from submission.

**What it costs in practice:** Property (€150,000+), transfer tax (3% of the assessed value = €4,500+), notary and registration fees (€500-1,500), lawyer (€500-1,000 for the residence application), bank deposit (€3,650, refundable), permit fee (€25), document translation and apostille (€200-400).

### Path C: Temporary Residence via Pension/Income

**What it is:** A one-year temporary residence permit based on demonstrating sufficient means of subsistence — pension income, Social Security, or savings.

**Who it's for:** Retirees who don't want to buy property but can document regular income.

**Key details:**
- No formal minimum pension amount is written in the law, but in practice, immigration officers look for evidence of €500-1,000/month in regular income, plus the €3,650 bank deposit.
- You need a rental contract for your accommodation (not just a hotel booking — a 12-month lease registered with the tax authority).
- Same renewal path as property-based residence: renewable annually, permanent residency after 5 years.

**Required documents:**
- Valid passport (all pages copied)
- Registered rental contract or proof of accommodation
- Pension statements, Social Security award letter, or bank statements showing regular income
- Bank statement from a Montenegrin bank showing €3,650 deposit
- Valid health insurance (minimum 30 days coverage)
- Criminal record certificate (within 6 months, translated)
- Proof of paying the €25 permit fee

**Processing time:** 30 days.

**The practical reality:** This path is less formalized than the property route. The income threshold is not spelled out in the law — it's at the discretion of the local police officer reviewing your application. Working with a Montenegrin immigration lawyer (€500-1,000) is strongly recommended. Approval rates vary by municipality.

## Tax Implications of Staying 183+ Days

If you spend 183 or more days in Montenegro within a calendar year, you become a tax resident. Montenegro taxes worldwide income for tax residents at progressive rates:

- First €700/month: **0%**
- €701-1,000/month: **9%**
- Above €1,000/month: **15%**

Plus a municipal surtax of up to 13-15% on the tax amount.

**Example:** A retiree with €2,000/month in combined Social Security and pension pays approximately €195-230/month in Montenegrin tax — an effective rate of about 10-12%. The U.S.-Montenegro double taxation treaty may reduce this further. Compare that to Portugal (14.5-48%) or Spain (19-47%) on the same income.

If you stay under 183 days — using the 90-day visa-free period or a D visa — you are not a Montenegrin tax resident and owe no Montenegrin income tax.

## Practical Comparison

### 90-day visa-free vs. D visa

**What the evidence says:**
The visa-free entry is free and requires no advance application — just show up with a passport. The D visa extends your stay to 6 months but requires a consulate application at least 60 days before travel, costs €50-100, and doesn't count toward permanent residency.

**What to check next:**
If you want 4-6 months per year in Montenegro, the D visa is the simplest path. If you want permanent residency eventually, skip the D visa and go directly to a temporary residence permit.

### Property route vs. income route

**What the evidence says:**
The property route (€150,000 minimum) is clearer, more predictable, and faster to process. The income route is cheaper upfront but less codified — approval depends on the local police officer's judgment, and the income threshold is not legally defined.

**What to check next:**
If you're buying property anyway, use it as your residence basis. If you're renting, get a lawyer to handle the income-based application and be prepared for the possibility that the first application is rejected.

### Montenegro vs. Portugal (D7 visa) vs. Spain (non-lucrative visa)

**What the evidence says:**
Montenegro's residence permit costs €25 and processes in 30 days. Portugal's D7 visa costs €90 and takes 60-120 days. Spain's non-lucrative visa costs €80 and takes 30-60 days. Montenegro has no minimum income threshold written in law; Portugal requires roughly €9,120/year; Spain requires roughly €28,800/year. Montenegro's pension tax rate (0-15%) is the lowest of the three.

**What to check next:**
Montenegro's system is simpler and cheaper, but less established. Portugal and Spain have decades of expat infrastructure, larger English-speaking communities, and more predictable healthcare. Montenegro is catching up but is not there yet.

## The Trade-Off

**What you may gain:**
- 90 days visa-free with zero paperwork — the easiest Adriatic entry for Americans
- A residence permit that costs €25 and processes in 30 days (vs. months in Portugal or Spain)
- Pension tax rates of 0-15% — among the lowest in Europe
- No minimum-stay requirement on property-based residence — live there 2 weeks or 12 months
- EU accession candidate using the euro — potential upside if Montenegro joins by 2028

**What you may give up:**
- A residency system that's less formalized than Western Europe — rules change frequently and enforcement varies by municipality
- Limited consular infrastructure for Americans (one embassy in Washington, D.C.)
- Healthcare that handles basics but requires travel for complex care
- The income-based residence path is discretionary, not rules-based
- No formal retiree visa — you're using general-purpose immigration paths

## The Golden Horizons 30-Day Test

1. Enter on your US passport — no visa needed. Register at the local police station or Turistički Info Centar within 24 hours (or confirm your hotel did it). Keep the white card receipt.
2. Open a bank account at a Montenegrin bank (CKB, Erste, NLB) — you'll need it for the €3,650 deposit if you pursue residence. Bring your passport and a utility bill or rental contract. Most banks in Kotor, Tivat, and Podgorica have English-speaking staff.
3. If testing the property route: visit a real estate agent and ask for properties with a tax-assessed value of €150,000+. Compare assessed values to asking prices — they often diverge significantly.
4. If testing the income route: bring your Social Security award letter, recent pension statements, and 3 months of bank statements. Meet with an immigration lawyer (budget €200-500 for a consultation) to assess whether your documentation will satisfy the local police.
5. Visit the local police station (Uprava Policije) where you'd submit your residence application. Ask about current processing times and any additional documents they require — requirements can vary by municipality.
6. Track your days carefully. Set a calendar reminder for day 80 — that gives you 10 days to leave if you haven't started a residence application. Overstaying is not worth the risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just leave Montenegro for a day and reset my 90 days?

No. The 90-day limit is calculated within a rolling 180-day window. Crossing into Croatia for lunch does not reset the clock — it just pauses it. If you've used 89 days and leave for one day, you return with 1 day remaining, not 90. You must wait until the 180-day window rolls over to get a fresh 90-day allowance. ([gov.me](https://www.gov.me/en/article/stay-of-up-to-90-days))

### What happens if I overstay the 90-day limit?

You face a fine (roughly €8 per day of overstay after a 24-hour grace period), potential deportation, and a re-entry ban of up to one year. The overstay is recorded in Montenegro's border database and will flag future visa or residence applications for additional scrutiny — even if the overstay was accidental. ([thingstodoinmontenegro.com](https://thingstodoinmontenegro.com/entry-requirements/))

### How much does a temporary residence permit actually cost?

The permit fee itself is €25. The real costs are around it: €3,650 bank deposit (refundable), €500-1,000 for an immigration lawyer, €200-400 for translated and apostilled documents, and health insurance (€70-200/month for private coverage). Total first-year cost excluding property: roughly €1,500-2,500. With a property purchase, add the purchase price plus 3% transfer tax. ([montenegrodigitalnomad.com](https://montenegrodigitalnomad.com/residency-in-montenegro/))

### Do I need to register with the police even if I'm just visiting for a week?

Yes. Every foreign visitor must register their address within 24 hours of arrival — the white card (prijava boravka). Hotels do this automatically. If you're staying in a private apartment, Airbnb, or with friends, you or your host must register in person. Failure to register can result in a €200-600 fine at the border when you leave. ([triplinkhub.com](https://www.triplinkhub.com/en/blog/montenegro-tourist-tax-white-card-registration))

### Will Montenegro's visa rules change when it joins the EU?

Montenegro has been an EU candidate since 2010 and is targeting accession by 2028, though no firm date is set. If Montenegro joins, Americans would fall under Schengen rules — likely 90 days within the Schengen zone (not 90 days in Montenegro specifically). This would significantly reduce flexibility for Americans who currently split time between Montenegro and Schengen countries. The January 2026 amendments to the Law on Foreigners already align Montenegro's visa policy closer to EU standards. ([minicondos.me](https://minicondos.me/new-law-on-foreigners-in-montenegro-2026/))
