# Medicare Part B and Moving Abroad: Should You Keep It? (Mexico City Cost Context)

Golden Horizons Status: PERSONAL DECISION — VERIFY BEFORE CANCELING

> THE SHORT ANSWER: You can generally keep Medicare Part B while living abroad, but it usually will not pay for routine care in Mexico City. Keeping it preserves your Part B enrollment for covered U.S. care. Dropping it can save premiums now, but may mean waiting to re-enroll and paying a lasting late-enrollment penalty. In 2026, the standard premium is $202.90 a month, or $2,434.80 a year, before any income-related surcharge or penalty. Before canceling, ask Social Security exactly when you could re-enroll, when coverage would begin, and whether an exception applies to you.

Imagine that you have settled into Mexico City. You have found an apartment, a doctor, and a routine you enjoy. Every month, you also pay for Medicare Part B—coverage that generally does not pay your local medical bills.

It is reasonable to ask what that premium is buying.

The answer is not routine healthcare in Mexico. It is continued enrollment in the U.S. medical coverage you may want if you visit, return permanently, or change your plans.

That does not make keeping Part B the right decision for everyone. It makes cancellation a decision about future access, enrollment rules, and financial risk—not simply whether local consultations appear affordable.

The most important question is not, “Will I use Part B this month?”

It is, “If I drop it and later need it, what happens next?”

## Retirement Snapshot

Primary decision:  
Whether the cost of keeping Part B is worthwhile given your U.S. return plans, other coverage, finances, and tolerance for a possible enrollment gap.

Best fit for keeping it:  
Someone who values continued Part B enrollment, expects covered U.S. outpatient care, or wants to preserve flexibility if the move does not work out.

Biggest warning:  
Returning to the United States does not mean you can automatically switch canceled Part B coverage back on immediately.

Planning-budget lens:  
The 2026 standard premium is $202.90 monthly. Your actual premium may be higher. Budget separately for Mexican healthcare and any U.S. deductibles, coinsurance, or supplemental coverage.

Residency:  
Mexican immigration permission and Medicare enrollment are separate systems. Confirm the residence process through the appropriate Mexican consular channel.

Healthcare:  
You need a plan for local treatment and emergencies in Mexico regardless of whether you retain Part B.

Housing / daily life:  
Choose an address based partly on practical access to the providers you intend to use—not only cafés, rent, or neighborhood reputation.

Safety:  
Review current official travel guidance for your destination and intended routes. This article does not assign a safety rating to a Mexico City neighborhood.

## Residency and Healthcare Realities for an American Retiree Considering Mexico City

Start by separating three decisions:

1. How you will legally live in Mexico.
2. How you will obtain and pay for care in Mexico.
3. Whether you will retain Medicare coverage for covered U.S. care.

An answer to one does not settle the others.

A Mexican residence document is not a healthcare policy. A local policy does not automatically preserve your Medicare re-enrollment rights. Keeping Part B does not establish a payment arrangement at a Mexican hospital.

Build the local healthcare plan around your actual needs.

If you take regular prescriptions, confirm the medicine, formulation, prescribing arrangements, and cost. If you see a specialist, identify an appropriate provider and ask about availability before moving.

For private insurance, obtain a quote for your age, medical history, and intended residence. Review:

- Pre-existing-condition exclusions.
- Waiting periods.
- Deductibles and coinsurance.
- Annual or other benefit limits.
- Provider networks.
- Direct billing versus reimbursement.
- Emergency treatment and evacuation provisions.
- Renewal conditions.

For self-pay care, request written estimates for relevant services and ask what payment is required before treatment. A consultation price does not establish the cost of an admission or emergency.

Do not choose a hospital because it appears in a directory or because a general article calls it affordable. Verify the facility, location, services, and payment arrangements yourself.

A scouting stay can help test everyday access. It cannot prove that every future medical need will be covered.

## Medicare Part B Considerations, Cost Context, and Practical Steps

### What Part B does—and does not—preserve

Part B helps cover physician services, outpatient care, medical equipment, and other eligible services. Part A generally addresses inpatient hospital coverage. Keeping Part B alone is not the same as maintaining every part of a complete U.S. healthcare arrangement.

Original Medicare generally does not cover care outside the United States. Narrow foreign-care exceptions exist, but they are not a routine healthcare plan for living in Mexico City.

If Part B remains active, it can help pay for eligible services when you receive covered care in the United States. Deductibles, coinsurance, provider participation, and any supplemental coverage still matter.

Source: [Current Medicare handbook](https://www.medicare.gov/publications/10050-medicare-and-you0.pdf).

### What keeping it costs

The standard Part B premium for 2026 is $202.90 monthly.

That equals $2,434.80 for 12 months at that rate. Your actual premium may be higher because of income-related adjustments or an existing late-enrollment penalty. Premiums can change annually.

You pay the premium even in months when you receive no Part B-covered services.

Use the amount on your own notice or benefit statement when building the budget. If the premium is already deducted from the Social Security income you enter as spendable income, do not subtract it again.

Source: [Medicare costs](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/medicare-basics/what-does-medicare-cost).

### The penalty you need to understand before dropping it

Without an applicable exception, the general Part B late-enrollment penalty is an additional 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not.

The penalty generally continues for as long as you have Part B. It is not a one-time reinstatement charge.

For example, Medicare’s published illustration shows that two full years of delayed enrollment can produce a 20% penalty. The penalty is calculated against the applicable standard premium, so its dollar amount can change when that premium changes.

Ask Social Security how the rule applies to your enrollment history before making a cancellation decision.

Source: [Medicare late-enrollment penalties](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs/avoid-penalties).

### You may also face a gap in coverage

If you do not qualify for a Special Enrollment Period or another applicable enrollment opportunity, you may need to use the General Enrollment Period: January 1 through March 31.

Under current rules, coverage starts the month after you sign up.

For example, someone who needs Part B in September but cannot enroll until the following January could face several months without it. A January enrollment generally begins February 1.

Do not confuse this with the October 15–December 7 period for changing Medicare health and drug plans. That is not a general opportunity to restart canceled Part B coverage.

Sources: [When Medicare coverage starts](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-does-medicare-coverage-start) and [Medicare Open Enrollment](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/open-enrollment).

### Living abroad does not automatically create an exception

One important Special Enrollment Period concerns qualifying group coverage based on your or your spouse’s current employment.

Personal insurance, retiree coverage, or simply living abroad should not be assumed to qualify you for that employment-based protection. Other special situations exist, including particular overseas volunteer circumstances, but they have their own conditions.

Ask Social Security to identify the specific enrollment rule that applies to you. “I will have insurance in Mexico” is not enough information to establish the answer.

Sources: [Medicare enrollment periods](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-does-medicare-coverage-start) and [Social Security’s Special Enrollment Period guidance](https://www.ssa.gov/help/iClaim_medSEP.html).

### How cancellation works

Do not simply stop paying.

Medicare directs people who want to drop Part B to contact Social Security. A signed written request is usually required.

Coverage generally ends at the end of the month after the month you file the request. Medicare’s example says a request filed April 5 would end coverage May 31.

Confirm your own termination date and keep the official notice.

Source: [How to drop Part A and Part B](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/ready-to-sign-up-for-part-a-part-b/how-to-drop-part-a-part-b).

## Practical Comparison

### Keeping Part B for covered U.S. care

What the evidence says:  
You continue paying premiums, but preserve Part B enrollment for covered services. Ordinary Medicare cost-sharing still applies.

What to check next:  
Review your U.S. providers, expected visits, supplemental arrangements, and actual premium. Consider an unplanned return as well as scheduled travel.

### Dropping Part B while abroad

What the evidence says:  
Cancellation can reduce current premiums, but may create a future enrollment gap and a continuing late-enrollment penalty.

What to check next:  
Ask Social Security when coverage would end, which enrollment opportunity would let you return, and whether a penalty would apply.

### Relying on Mexican insurance or self-pay care

What the evidence says:  
A local healthcare arrangement addresses a different need from continued Part B enrollment. Having it does not, by itself, establish an exemption from Medicare enrollment rules.

What to check next:  
Obtain approved policy terms or a realistic self-pay and emergency-funding plan. Ask Social Security separately about your Medicare rights.

### Keeping Medicare Advantage, Part D, or Medigap

What the evidence says:  
These are not interchangeable with Part B. Medicare Advantage and drug plans have their own enrollment and service-area rules, while supplemental coverage has separate conditions.

What to check next:  
Tell each plan or insurer about the intended move and ask what happens to eligibility, coverage, premiums, and future enrollment. Do not assume keeping Part B preserves every other policy.

### Choosing where to live in Mexico City

What the evidence says:  
Citywide living-cost data do not establish neighborhood rent, accessibility, specialist availability, or emergency response.

What to check next:  
Compare actual apartments and practical journeys to appropriate providers. Choose an address around your needs rather than a generic neighborhood recommendation.

## The Trade-Off

What keeping Part B may preserve:

- Continued Part B enrollment.
- Access to benefits for covered U.S. services.
- Flexibility if the move abroad ends earlier than expected.
- Avoidance of a cancellation-related enrollment gap and additional penalty exposure while coverage remains active.

What keeping it requires:

- Continuing premiums even when you do not use covered services.
- A separate plan for ordinary care in Mexico.
- Budgeting for U.S. cost-sharing and any additional coverage.
- Reviewing the arrangement when your plans or premiums change.

What dropping it may save:

- Future Part B premiums after the confirmed termination date.

What dropping it may expose you to:

- A wait before re-enrollment.
- A period without Part B during a return to the United States.
- A late-enrollment penalty if no applicable exception protects you.
- The need to reconsider other coverage arrangements.

Neither column settles the decision on its own. The right comparison is the money saved now against the access and financial risks you would accept later.

## The Golden Horizons 30-Day Test

1. Identify your actual coverage.  
   List Part A, Part B, Medicare Advantage, Part D, Medigap, employer or retiree coverage, and any international policy you hold.

2. Record the real cost.  
   Use current notices to identify premiums and avoid counting deductions twice in your retirement budget.

3. Ask Social Security about your specific scenario.  
   Confirm the cancellation date, re-enrollment route, possible penalty, and coverage start date. Ask what official documentation or written explanation is available.

4. Establish a local care plan.  
   Contact appropriate Mexico City providers and insurers. Investigate your prescriptions, ongoing treatment, emergency options, exclusions, and payment requirements.

5. Test an unplanned return.  
   Ask what would happen if you needed U.S. outpatient care next month—not just during a future January visit. Identify any gap in the proposed arrangement.

6. Make a documented decision.  
   Write down why you are keeping or dropping Part B, what remains uncertain, and when you will review the choice.

This is a research framework, not a deadline to cancel coverage. You do not need to complete it in exactly 30 days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I keep Medicare Part B if I move to Mexico City?

Generally, yes. You can continue paying for Part B while living abroad. It usually does not pay for routine Mexican care, but continued enrollment can matter for covered care in the United States.

### What is the Part B premium?

The standard 2026 premium is $202.90 monthly. Your actual amount may be higher because of income-related adjustments or penalties. Check the applicable year and your own notice.

### Can I drop it and restart as soon as I return?

Not necessarily. If you lack an applicable Special Enrollment Period or other enrollment opportunity, you may need to wait for January 1–March 31. Coverage generally starts the month after enrollment, and a penalty may apply.

### Does Mexican insurance protect me from the Part B penalty?

Do not assume so. The employment-based Special Enrollment Period has specific requirements. Ask Social Security whether your particular coverage and circumstances qualify under an applicable rule.

### How large is the late-enrollment penalty?

The general rule adds 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not, unless an exception applies. The penalty generally lasts as long as you have Part B.

### Does keeping Part B mean a medical evacuation will be covered?

Do not assume it does. Original Medicare generally does not cover foreign care, and keeping Part B does not establish an international evacuation policy. Check evacuation terms separately with any relevant insurer.

### Should I cancel Part B before my scouting trip?

Do not treat a short research visit as a reason to make a permanent coverage change. First establish your local travel coverage and understand the consequences of cancellation.

Golden Horizons Verdict: PERSONAL DECISION — VERIFY BEFORE CANCELING

Keeping Part B can preserve valuable flexibility for covered U.S. care, but it does not replace a healthcare plan in Mexico.

Dropping it can reduce current expenses, but the decision is not easily reversible on demand. Understand the enrollment window, possible gap, and penalty before treating the premium as money you can safely redirect elsewhere.

It is knowing what each choice costs—and what would happen if your retirement plans changed.
