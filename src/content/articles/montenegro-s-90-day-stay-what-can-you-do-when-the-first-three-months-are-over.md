# Montenegro’s 90-Day Stay: What Can You Do When the First Three Months Are Over?

> **THE SHORT ANSWER:** Americans can generally spend up to 90 days in Montenegro within a 180-day period, then must leave or obtain another legal status. In Podgorica, a 1-bedroom can run about $406-$1,137, utilities about $78-$101, internet about $28, a transit pass about $32, and a cappuccino about $2.39. These September 2026 figures give you a useful test budget, but a longer stay depends on your purpose and a formal permit application.

The 90th day can arrive quietly. You may have found a café you like, learned your way around the grocery store, and started picturing what an ordinary year in Montenegro might feel like. Then the calendar becomes the most important document in your bag.

Montenegro can give you a meaningful three-month look at daily life. But a visitor stay is not a trial run that simply rolls into permanent life. If Podgorica begins to feel less like a stop and more like a possibility, the next decision is legal first: leave when required, or pursue the formal status that fits your circumstances.

## When the Visitor Clock Runs Out

What happens when your first three months are over? The legal answer is straightforward: a visitor stay is generally limited to **90 days within 180 days**. Once those days are used, you must leave Montenegro or hold another lawful status before staying longer.

That makes the entry date more than a stamp in your passport. It is the date around which your plans need to be built. Keep a simple calendar from day 1 through day 90, along with your entry record and accommodation confirmations.

### Three sensible ways to think about the next step

The first possibility is departure. But a brief trip outside Montenegro does not automatically reset the clock; earlier days may still sit inside the same 180-day period.

The second is a formal temporary-residence route. Montenegro’s requirements can depend on why you plan to stay, including family circumstances, work, or another recognized basis. The official visitor guidance does not offer one universal retirement permit or one standard income requirement, so ask the responsible Montenegrin authority which route matches your own situation.

The third is to treat the original 90 days as exactly what they are: a careful scouting period. By day 30, you may know whether Centar suits your daily rhythm. By day 60, you can have real housing and insurance notes rather than assumptions. By day 90, you can decide whether the paperwork is worth pursuing.

### Start early, while you still have choices

Do not wait until day 89 to investigate a longer stay. Contact the appropriate Montenegrin authority while you still have time to understand the permit category, required documents, likely processing questions, and whether applying changes your right to remain.

Bring a simple folder: your passport, entry record, accommodation confirmations, residence-purpose notes, health-insurance certificate, and any documents the authority names for your permit category. The Government of Montenegro’s visitor page confirms the 90-in-180-day rule, but it does not publish one processing timeline for every residence path.

Housing may become part of that conversation. Central Point Nekretnine and other Podgorica agencies show how much furnished 1-bedroom choices can vary by building, location, and contract terms, particularly between Centar and nearby districts.

The visitor stay may feel simple. Longer residence asks more of you—but it also gives you the chance to make a decision based on a lived-in routine rather than a beautiful first impression.

Look through current Central Point Nekretnine listings, but do not stop at the advertised rent. Compare at least 3 listings using the same questions: Is it furnished? What is the heating method? Is internet included or separate? What lease length is available?

Utilities deserve the same attention. Basic electricity, heating, cooling, water, and garbage run about $78-$101 for a small apartment, while internet is about $28. Ask to see a recent bill before agreeing to a lease, especially if you will arrive in a different season than the current tenant.

Then test how you move through the city. A monthly public-transit pass is about $32, while an 8-kilometer taxi ride is about $7. Take the bus for several days, then use a taxi on an evening when you would rather not walk. That tells you more about your likely routine than a map ever will.

## Healthcare Is the Part to Settle Before You Stay Longer

Healthcare is one of the first realities to clarify before pursuing longer residence. Euraxess Montenegro states that foreigners need health insurance for access to non-emergency state care. Urgent or uncovered services, meanwhile, may mean paying out of pocket.

During your first month, identify the Clinical Centre of Montenegro. Ask how foreign patients are received, what insurance documents staff need, and what language support may be available. These are not dramatic questions; they are the questions that make an unfamiliar place feel manageable.

Docta.me is another local healthcare resource to contact when comparing private-care options. Ask for written information about a basic general-practitioner visit, accepted insurance, appointment availability, and English-language support.

Private clinics may offer another option for routine care, but accepted insurance networks and price schedules can vary. Request written information about a basic general-practitioner visit from the Clinical Centre of Montenegro and a private provider, then compare it with the terms of an international or locally available policy.

The International Organization for Migration’s Montenegro country information notes that coverage and eligibility can differ for visitors and temporary residents. Keep your passport copy, policy certificate, emergency contact number, and insurer claims instructions in one place.

Insurance may not be the romantic part of imagining life abroad. It is, however, the part that helps a quiet 90-day experiment remain calm when life becomes ordinary.
