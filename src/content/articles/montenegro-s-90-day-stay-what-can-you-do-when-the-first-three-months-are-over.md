# Montenegro’s 90-Day Stay: What Can You Do When the First Three Months Are Over?

> **THE SHORT ANSWER:** After 90 days, many American visitors must leave Montenegro or move into a long-stay visa or residence-permit route. In Podgorica, a one-bedroom runs about $619-$1,128 per month, utilities plus internet cost about $106-$128, a monthly transit pass is about $31, and a neighborhood dinner for two is about $24. The key question is whether you can document the requirements for staying past day 90.

A $619 one-bedroom, a $31 monthly transit pass, and a neighborhood dinner for two around $24 can make Podgorica look like a very appealing place to linger. But Montenegro’s first three months are not an open-ended invitation. They are a useful, finite window to discover whether the rhythm of daily life suits you—and whether you can legally remain once day 90 arrives.

## When Day 90 Changes the Conversation

The first 90 days are straightforward enough to picture: a temporary apartment, errands on foot, coffee at a café, perhaps a growing sense that you could settle into the city. Then the calendar changes the question.

Montenegro’s government provides a short-stay framework of up to 90 days, with re-entry counted under a 180-day rule. If you hope to remain after that initial period, you need to establish whether a long-stay visa or residence-permit option fits your passport and circumstances.

The official visa overview confirms that longer-stay routes exist. It does not publish one fixed retirement-income or savings threshold for every applicant. It also does not give one universal document list or processing timeline.

Before you book month 4, read the government’s short-stay guidance and visa-regime overview. Then contact the Montenegrin consular office serving your state with focused questions about passport eligibility, proof of funds, accommodation evidence, background-check information, insurance, where you must apply, renewal terms, and current processing time.

### Your document checklist for a longer-stay inquiry

Start gathering the items Montenegro’s official framework and the research memo identify as central to a long-stay application:

- A valid passport.
- Proof of funds or income.
- Evidence of accommodation, such as a rental agreement.
- Background-check information.
- Proof of health insurance.
- The correct application forms and the office where you must file.
- Current information on renewal terms and processing time.

The Government of Montenegro confirms that long-stay options exist, but the memo does not provide a single exact income or savings threshold. Ask the consular office for the current amount that applies to your route before you sign a lease or plan beyond day 90.

The memo also does not document Montenegro’s tax-treaty status with the United States. Keep that question separate from your visa plan, and get advice from a U.S. tax professional before changing your tax residence or drawing income abroad.

### Treat the first three months as a scouting window

During days 1 through 30, learn the ordinary rhythm. A coffee at an expat-area café is about $2-$3; a casual business-district lunch is about $9-$13; a local transport fare is about $1-$2. Those small routines tell you more about daily comfort than a week of sightseeing can.

During days 31 through 60, test housing. Compare a normal-area apartment near everyday shops with a more expensive area, and ask landlords whether a longer rental arrangement can provide the formal documentation a residence application may require.

During days 61 through 90, turn from browsing to verification. Confirm your nationality’s current rules, the document list, proof-of-funds expectations, insurance treatment, and whether an application must be made before your permitted stay ends.

Montenegro may invite you to slow down over a $2 coffee. The legal plan cannot wait until the last week.

## What Everyday Podgorica Costs Can—and Cannot—Tell You

Podgorica is not a coastal fantasy priced for a summer holiday. It is a capital city where the more revealing questions are ordinary ones: What will the apartment cost? Where will you buy milk? Can you get around easily? What happens when utility bills arrive?

A one-bedroom in a normal Podgorica area is about $619 per month. In a more expensive Podgorica area, the comparable anchor rises to about $1,128. Expatistan’s figures are crowd-sourced, so treat them as a useful starting range—not a landlord’s offer. Still, the $509 spread is meaningful. It can determine whether the city feels like a modest experiment or a tighter monthly commitment.

Utilities for a smaller one-person home are about $78 per month, while a larger two-person setup is around $100. Internet adds about $28, putting those basic home bills near $106-$128 before a mobile plan, insurance, or personal extras.

The grocery anchors are modest, though the shop nearest your apartment will be the price that matters. A liter of milk is about $1.49, a dozen large eggs about $4.03, tomatoes about $2.40 per kilogram, apples about $1.79 per kilogram, potatoes about $1.04 per kilogram, and daily bread for two about $0.86.

Getting around can remain inexpensive if public transport works for your routine. A monthly pass is about $31, while an 8-kilometer taxi ride is about $7 and a liter of gasoline about $1.80. The difference between a transit-based life and a taxi-dependent one can quietly reshape the month.

Dining is where a pleasant routine can become a larger expense. A modest dinner for two at a neighborhood pub is about $24; dinner for two with wine and dessert at an Italian restaurant in an expat area is about $48. Both can fit into a satisfying life. Neither should be mistaken for a necessary cost.

## Healthcare and Safety: The Questions to Ask Before You Need Them

Montenegro has public and private healthcare, but a 90-day entry stamp does not automatically create the same access as residence status. Before relying on any plan, ask both your insurer and the provider exactly what is covered for a non-resident American.

In Podgorica, Clinical Center of Montenegro is the major public institution to ask about public-system access and emergency arrangements. Codra Hospital is a private provider worth contacting about appointment availability, accepted international insurance, English-language care, and up-front payment expectations.

The memo does not provide verified local prices for a private GP visit, specialist consultation, emergency care, prescriptions, or imaging. That absence matters more than a favorable rent figure. Ask Clinical Center of Montenegro and Codra Hospital for written information before deciding that a $1,200-$2,000 planning range will meet your health needs.

Ask an insurer such as Lovćen osiguranje whether its policies are available for your status and whether private facilities bill the plan directly. Also confirm whether any existing international policy covers Montenegro for the full 90-day period and a proposed long-stay transition.

Medicare generally does not cover healthcare received outside the United States. Keep Medicare decisions separate from your Montenegro insurance plan, and think through the care you would need if you returned to the United States after 30, 60, or 90 days.

Safety is best measured during regular errands rather than from a broad national description. Ride the $1-$2 local bus after dark. Take a $7 taxi for an 8-kilometer trip. Walk from a possible apartment to a grocery shop and notice how the route feels at the hours you would actually use it.
